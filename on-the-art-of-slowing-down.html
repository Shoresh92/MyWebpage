On the Art of Slowing Down

A reflection on what happens when you stop rushing and start noticing — the small moments that make a life worth examining.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes not from doing too much, but from never truly stopping. We have become so fluent in the language of productivity that stillness feels like a foreign dialect — one we once knew but have slowly, quietly forgotten.

I noticed it first in the mornings. I had taken to checking my phone before my feet touched the floor. Before coffee, before light, before even a full breath — the world was already pouring in. The day began not with me, but with everyone else.

The fastest way to lose yourself is to keep moving without ever asking where you're going.

The Permission We Never Give Ourselves

Slowing down, I've come to believe, is not a luxury. It is not something you earn after the inbox is cleared or the deadlines are met. Those conditions never fully arrive. Slowness has to be chosen — stubbornly, repeatedly, against the current.

It starts with small things. Leaving your phone in another room. Eating without a screen. Walking somewhere without headphones. In code, it looks something like this:

def slow_down(thoughts):
    for thought in thoughts:
        print(thought)
        time.sleep(1)  # pause between each one

slow_down(["breathe", "notice", "be here"])

These feel almost transgressive at first — as if you are breaking a social contract no one explicitly signed but everyone somehow agreed to. But then something shifts. The world doesn't collapse. In fact, it sharpens.

What You Find in the Stillness

I won't pretend the stillness is always comfortable. Sometimes what rises is exactly what the busyness was designed to keep submerged: grief, longing, questions without easy answers.

async function slowDown(thoughts) {
  for (const thought of thoughts) {
    console.log(thought);
    await new Promise(r => setTimeout(r, 1000));
  }
}

slowDown(["breathe", "notice", "be here"]);

On the other side of that discomfort is something worth having. Clarity. A sense of your own rhythm. The quiet knowledge of what actually matters to you, as distinct from what the world insists should. You can even reference inline concepts like this within regular sentences.

We are, most of us, moving too fast to find out who we are when we stop. That might be worth changing.
